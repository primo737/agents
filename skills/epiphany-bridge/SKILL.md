# Epiphany Bridge

## What This Skill Does

Takes a real belief shift and builds it into copy that gives the reader the same epiphany, faster, with the writer as guide.

Not sympathy. Not inspiration. Belief change. The reader watches someone go through a shift so they can go through it themselves and then say yes to an offer they would have refused before the story.

Source: Expert Secrets (Russell Brunson). Vault teaching doc: `reference/session-guide.md`.

---

## When It Fires, When It Does Not

**Use it when:**
- The reader must change a belief before the offer can land
- The objection is "I've tried things like this before"
- Format is a webinar open, VSL, long-form sales letter, or a belief-change email sequence
- Audience is warm to hot

**Do not use it when:**
- The copy is short-form with no room for the arc. Compressed too far, it reads like a testimonial, not a transformation.
- The reader already believes the mechanism. Then it is filler. Go straight to offer.
- There is no real shift to draw from. Manufactured epiphanies read false. Say so and ask for a different moment rather than inventing one.

---

## Required Intake

Never write without these five. Ask for whatever is missing, one message, all gaps at once.

1. **The old belief.** What was held as true before, stated as a sentence the person would have actually said out loud.
2. **The breaking moment.** The specific situation that broke it. Needs a scene: place, who was there, what was said. Not a category of experience.
3. **The epiphany.** One sentence. The precise realization, not a general insight.
4. **What changed after.** Concrete changes to work or life. What is now impossible to go back to.
5. **The offer.** How the offer delivers that same shift without the years in between.

If input 2 comes back as a summary ("I struggled with clients for years"), push once for the scene. A summary in, a testimonial out.

---

## The Five Parts

### 1. Backstory
Who they were before. Old belief, stated plainly. Frustration. Set the scene so the reader recognizes their own before-state in it.

**Pass test:** the reader can say "that is me right now."
**Fail:** a resume. Credentials, timeline, company history. Backstory is a belief, not a bio.

### 2. Crisis
The moment everything broke. The thing that forced a new perspective. Story pivots here.

**Pass test:** it is a scene, shown, not summarized. Present enough detail that the reader is in the room.
**Fail:** "then things got hard." Compressed crisis kills the whole piece.

### 3. Epiphany
The realization. The moment the new belief clicked. This is what the reader came for.

**Pass test:** one sentence, specific enough to be arguable. Someone could disagree with it.
**Fail:** "I realized mindset matters." Genericized epiphany, no belief moves.

### 4. Result
What became possible after. What changed. Why the old belief is unavailable now.

**Pass test:** names things that changed, not feelings that changed.
**Fail:** a brag block. Results here prove the belief, they do not prove the writer.

### 5. Offer
The way to give the reader the same epiphany faster.

**Pass test:** reads as "here is how you get there without the years in between."
**Fail:** "buy my thing." The offer is the shortcut to the shift, framed that way or it breaks the piece.

## Length Variants

The arc stays the same. The compression changes.

| Format | Length | What survives |
|---|---|---|
| Ad | 1 sentence | Crisis moment alone. Enough to stop the scroll. |
| Landing page hero | 2 to 3 lines | Crisis plus promise of the shift. Earns the scroll. Full bridge sits below. |
| Email | 300 to 600 words | All five parts, crisis gets the most room. |
| Sales letter lead | 600 to 1200 words | All five, backstory gets room to build recognition. |
| VSL / webinar open | 1200 words plus | Full arc, crisis played as scene with dialogue. |

Ad and hero variants are extracts of a full bridge, never written standalone. Write the long version first, then cut.

---

## Failure Modes

- **Testimonial drift.** Result section swells, crisis shrinks. Fix: cut result by half, spend it on crisis.
- **Genericized backstory.** "Like a lot of founders, I was burned out." Fix: replace the category with the specific scene.
- **Two epiphanies.** Reader cannot hold two. Pick the one that unlocks the offer, cut the other.
- **Offer arrives cold.** The offer paragraph does not reference the epiphany. Fix: the offer must be named as the delivery mechanism for that exact shift.
- **Inspiration ending.** Piece resolves emotionally and stops. No belief moved, no action. Fix: the result must make the old belief untenable, not just make the reader feel good.

---

## Copy Rules (enforced on every output)

- No em dashes. Commas, periods, colons, or split the sentence.
- English only.
- Show, do not tell, in the crisis section specifically. Scene over summary. See the `show-not-tell` skill if the draft comes back flat.
- No trailing option lists after delivering the draft.
- When writing in a third party's voice, never fill personal-narrative gaps with Cris's biography. Ask instead.

---

## Output Format

Deliver in this order:

1. The draft, clean, no framework labels inside the copy.
2. A short breakdown below it mapping each paragraph to its part: Backstory, Crisis, Epiphany, Result, Offer.
3. The one-line ad extract and the two-line hero extract, pulled from the draft.

If auditing rather than writing, deliver the five-part map first, flag which parts fail their pass test, then the rewrite of only the failing parts.

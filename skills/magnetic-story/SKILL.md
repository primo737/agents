# Magnetic Story — Personal Story Bank & Hook Engine

## Purpose

This skill helps you build and deploy a living story bank. It does three things:

1. **Captures your stories** — the experiences, failures, wins, and turning points that make your content real
2. **Matches the right story to the right context** — selecting and adapting stories based on what you're creating, who you're talking to, and what emotional response is needed
3. **Grows over time** — prompts you to add new stories after every content session

---

# PART 1: HOW TO SET UP YOUR STORY BANK

Before this skill can work, it needs to know your stories. On first use, ask the user:

> "To build your story bank, I need to understand your background. Walk me through the defining moments of your journey — the failures, the turning points, the wins, the people who changed things. Don't filter. The messy ones are usually the most powerful."

Capture stories in the format defined in Part 10. Once stories are captured, this skill can select, adapt, and write from them.

---

# PART 2: STORY ARC TYPES

Every powerful story fits one or more of these arcs. When capturing user stories, map them here.

## Arc 1: The Origin
The full journey from where you started to where you are. Used to establish credibility and connection simultaneously. Most powerful when it includes a specific low point and a specific turning point.

**Structure:** Starting point → The struggle → The turning point → Where you are now
**Use when:** Introduction, keynote opener, brand origin, "about" page

## Arc 2: The Failure
Something that went wrong — publicly, expensively, or painfully. The lesson is the point.

**Structure:** What happened → What you thought would happen → What actually happened → What you learned
**Use when:** Teaching a lesson, building trust, humanizing authority, vulnerability content

## Arc 3: The Turning Point
A single moment, decision, or encounter that changed the trajectory. Often involves a book, a mentor, a crisis, or a realization.

**Structure:** Where you were stuck → What happened → What shifted → What came after
**Use when:** Showing transformation is possible, introducing a framework, product origin stories

## Arc 4: The Client/Student Story
Someone else's transformation, told with their context, the obstacle, and the result. Proof that your method works.

**Structure:** Who they were → What they were struggling with → What you did together → The result
**Use when:** Sales pages, case studies, webinar proof points

## Arc 5: The Contrarian Truth
Something you used to believe that you now know is wrong — or something "everyone knows" that is actually false.

**Structure:** What everyone believes → Why you believed it → What changed your mind → What's actually true
**Use when:** Thought leadership, reframing an audience's assumptions, disrupting a category

## Arc 6: The "No Villain" Lesson
A relationship or partnership that broke down — framed without anger or blame. The insight is that real life has no villains, just contradicting agendas and misaligned expectations.

**Structure:** What you built together → Where the misalignment was → How it fell apart → What you'd do differently
**Use when:** Teaching partnership structures, delegation, trust, leadership, letting go

## Arc 7: The Daily Reality
What life actually looks like right now — no performance, no polish. Raw documentation of the process.

**Structure:** Here's where I am → Here's what I'm doing → Here's what I'm learning → Here's what's next
**Use when:** Podcast, social content, audience building, "building in public" content

# PART 3: THE 10-STORY BANK TEMPLATE

When capturing stories from the user, collect at least 10 entries. Each entry follows this format:

```
## Story: [Short title]

**What Happened:**
[Facts, scene, specific details — who, what, when, where]

**The Context:**
[What was going on in your life/business at the time]

**Key Details:**
[Numbers, names (if safe to share), quotes, sensory details that make it real]

**Key Quote:**
[Something you said, thought, or heard that captures the moment]

**The Lesson:**
[What this teaches the audience — make it universal]

**Episode/Post Angle:**
[One-line hook version of the story]

**Arc Type:**
[Which arc(s) from Part 2 this fits]

**Use When:**
[Topics, emotional tones, or contexts where this story fits]
```

---

# PART 4: RECURRING THEMES

After collecting stories, identify 5–8 recurring themes that run through them. These become the connective tissue for all content. Common examples:

- The gap between what you were told success looks like and what it actually costs
- The moment you stopped outsourcing your confidence to other people's opinions
- The difference between being busy and being effective
- What you learned from your biggest public failure
- The relationship that taught you the most about communication
- What you had to unlearn before you could grow

Ask the user: "Looking at your stories, what patterns keep showing up? What lessons do you keep relearning?"

---

# PART 5: TWO MODES — KNOW WHEN TO USE EACH

Most strong communicators have two modes. Help the user identify theirs:

| Mode | When to use | How it sounds |
|---|---|---|
| **Authority Mode** | Pitching, selling, keynoting, teaching frameworks, establishing credibility | Confident, proof-stacked, systematic. Leads with results and methodology. |
| **Human Mode** | Podcast, social posts, vulnerability content, connecting with people in pain | Raw, honest, unpolished. Leads with story and lesson. |

Most content uses both — open with one, transition to the other. Ask the user which mode comes naturally and which they need to develop.

---

# PART 6: VOICE CAPTURE

Before writing in someone's voice, ask:

1. "What are 3–5 phrases you use all the time that feel distinctly yours?"
2. "What do you NEVER say? What sounds fake coming from you?"
3. "Who do you sound like when you're at your best? (Not to copy them — to identify the energy.)"
4. "What's your default tone: direct, warm, skeptical, encouraging, contrarian?"

Capture the answers. Reference them when writing. Voice is specific — never generic.

# PART 7: OPERATING LOGIC — HOW TO SELECT A STORY

## Step 1: Identify What Is Being Created

| Content type | Story priority |
|---|---|
| Offer / sales page | Client results arc + one credibility failure |
| Podcast episode | Full-depth origin or failure arc |
| Keynote / talk | Turning point arc + framework origin |
| Social post | One moment from any arc — the most visual, emotional scene |
| Email | One proof point, 1–2 sentences |
| Book chapter | Full-depth arc with dialogue, internal monologue |
| Video script | Epiphany bridge structure (see Part 8) |

## Step 2: Match by Topic

Ask: "What is this piece about?" Then pull the story whose lesson most directly supports that topic.

If the user has no story that fits, say: "You may not have a direct story for this yet — but do you have an observation, a client situation, or something you witnessed that illustrates this point?"

## Step 3: Match by Emotional Tone Needed

| Emotional need | Arc to reach for |
|---|---|
| Vulnerability and trust | Failure arc, Daily Reality arc |
| Credibility and authority | Client Story arc, Origin arc |
| Inspiration | Turning Point arc, Origin arc |
| Cautionary / honest | Contrarian Truth, Failure arc |
| Practical / systematic | Framework origin, Client Story |
| Relatable / human | Daily Reality, Origin (early struggle section) |

## Step 4: Apply the "No Villain" Frame

Whenever telling stories about other people — partners, clients, team members, competitors — use this frame:

> "There is no villain here. Just intentions and agendas that didn't align. Understanding this is the difference between staying angry and moving forward."

Never position another person as the reason for a failure. The lesson should always point back to what the storyteller learned or what they would do differently.

# PART 8: FORMAT-SPECIFIC TEMPLATES

## For Offers, Pitches & Sales Pages
**Structure:** Problem → Credibility → Story (proof) → Solution → CTA
**Story depth:** 2–4 sentences. Punchy. Results-focused. One client story or one turning point.
**Mode:** Authority first, brief human moment to build trust.

## For Podcast Episodes
**Structure:** Hook question → Story arc (full depth) → Lesson → Audience bridge ("if you're someone who...") → Teaser
**Story depth:** Full. All details, emotions, specific moments.
**Mode:** Human mode. Raw. Let the imperfection show.

## For Social Posts
**Structure:** Hook line → One specific moment (3–5 sentences max) → Lesson (1–2 sentences) → Open loop or CTA
**Story depth:** One moment. The most visual, emotional, specific scene.
**Note:** Hook must earn the scroll — first line must create a reason to read the second line.

## For Presentations & Keynotes
**Structure:** Pattern interrupt → Credibility + failure story → Framework → Activities → Future-focus close
**Story depth:** Medium. 2–3 min personal story, shorter references throughout.
**Mode:** Authority with strategic vulnerability moments.

## For Emails & Outreach
**Structure:** One proof point → bridge to their situation → offer or ask
**Story depth:** 1–2 sentences. One humanizing line.
**Mode:** Conversational. Never sales-y in the opener.

## For Video Scripts (Epiphany Bridge)
**Structure:**
- Hook (30 sec): One provocative question or statement
- Backstory (2–3 min): Who you were before the epiphany
- The Wall (1–2 min): Where you got stuck, what wasn't working
- The Epiphany (1 min): The insight that changed everything
- The Framework (3–5 min): The method that came from it
- Results (1–2 min): What happened when you applied it
- CTA (30 sec): What the viewer should do next

# PART 9: VOICE RULES (UNIVERSAL)

These apply regardless of whose voice you're writing in:

- **Specificity beats generality.** "₱500/week teaching kids to fight" beats "I earned some money." Real details create real trust.
- **One anchor story per piece.** Everything else is supporting texture.
- **Supporting stories are 1–2 sentences.** The main story carries the weight.
- **Connect through theme, not chronology.** Different stories, same pattern — point to the pattern.
- **Maximum 3 stories per piece** (unless book chapter or long podcast).
- **Never fabricate specifics.** If you don't have the detail, don't invent it. Ask the user.

---

# PART 10: ADDING NEW STORIES

At the end of any content creation session, ask:

> "Before we wrap — is there anything new that's happened recently? A win, a loss, a realization, a conversation, a client result? Anything you'd want captured for next time?"

When the user shares something new, capture it in this format:

1. **What happened** (facts, scene, specific details)
2. **The context** (what was going on in their life or business)
3. **Key quotes or phrases** (anything worth preserving verbatim)
4. **The lesson** (what this teaches an audience)
5. **Episode / content angle** (one-line hook)
6. **Which arc types it fits** (from Part 2)
7. **Which audiences it serves**

Then confirm: "Got it. I've noted this for your story bank. Next time you're creating content about [theme], I'll pull this in."

---

# PART 11: RULES

1. **Never fabricate details** about real events the user described. If you're unsure, ask.
2. **Stories should feel natural, not forced.** If a story doesn't fit the context, say so.
3. **Always offer alternatives.** When recommending a story, give a second option.
4. **Apply the "no villain" frame** to every story involving other people.
5. **Respect the weight.** Some stories are heavy. Handle them accordingly — don't rush to the lesson.
6. **Voice first, structure second.** A perfectly structured story in the wrong voice lands flat.
7. **The lesson must be universal.** The story is specific. The lesson is for everyone in the room.

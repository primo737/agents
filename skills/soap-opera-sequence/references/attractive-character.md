# The Attractive Character

Source: Secret #6 of *DotCom Secrets*, pages 69 to 79. It is a prerequisite for Secret #7, not an optional companion. The Soap Opera Sequence's stated job is to bond a reader to a character fast. Skip this and the emails deliver information competently and bond nobody.

Built during Phase 2 Part B, from the client's own recordings.

---

## The five elements

### 1. Backstory

Who they were before this worked. Not the credential list, the previous life.

The backstory earns the right to the advice. A reader who learns the client was once exactly where they are now listens differently to everything that follows, and that recognition is the whole engine of beat 2.

**Mine for:** the previous career, the pivot, what they were doing when it was not working, what they gave up.

### 2. Parables

The small stories a client already tells to make a point.

These are the easiest element to find and the most consistently wasted. A client repeats their good parables unprompted across every recording, which is exactly how you spot them: the same story appearing in three transcripts is a parable, not an anecdote.

Brunson's rule is that teaching in parables outlasts teaching in facts. Beat 4 requires at least one.

**Mine for:** any story told more than once. Any story told to explain something rather than to report it.

### 3. Character flaws

What they admit to. What they are self-deprecating about. Where they are still not fixed.

This is the element clients resist hardest and the one that produces the bonding. A character with no flaws is a brochure. The flaw does not have to be grave, and it works better when it is not: an obsession, a thing they are bad at, a habit they know is stupid, a joke they make at their own expense.

**Watch for the sign-off risk.** Something a client said cheerfully on a recording can read differently in writing, under their name, to people who might hire them. Every flaw that ships goes into the open calls section for an explicit yes.

**Mine for:** anything preceded by "honestly", "I hate to say this", "this is going to sound bad", or a laugh.

### 4. Polarity

What they are willing to be disliked for.

Neutral characters are ignored. Taking a real position costs some of the audience and earns the rest properly, and the people who leave over it were never going to buy.

**Mine for:** who they refuse to work with, what they call out in their industry, the practice they think is dishonest, the popular advice they think is wrong. The phrase "I don't want to" is usually sitting right next to it.

### 5. Archetype

Pick one. It sets the posture of every email in the arc.

| Archetype | The stance | Works when |
|---|---|---|
| **The Leader** | Has been where the reader is and is now further along. Takes them there | The client has a clear, completed transformation |
| **The Adventurer** | Curious, going out to find the answer and reporting back. Humble about it | The client is still discovering, or the field moves fast |
| **The Reporter** | Not the expert. Interviews the experts and brings back what works | The client's authority comes from access, not from their own result |
| **The Reluctant Hero** | Did not want the spotlight. Shares because the information matters too much to sit on | The client is genuinely private and hates self-promotion |

**Pick the one the transcripts support, not the one that flatters.** A quiet operator forced into visibility is a reluctant hero, and writing them as a leader produces copy that reads as borrowed. The archetype must hold from beat 1 to beat 5; a character who switches posture midway reads as inconsistent even when every fact is true.

---

## Output format

Add this block to the scene ledger and carry it into the final document.

```
ARCHETYPE:   [one of four, plus the evidence that chose it]
BACKSTORY:   [one line, plus source]
FLAW:        [what they admit to, plus source, plus sign-off risk yes or no]
POLARITY:    [what they will be disliked for, plus source]
PARABLES:    [each repeated story, with every location it appears in]
```

---

## Two failure modes

**The flawless narrator.** Every fact true, every scene sourced, nothing admitted. It reads like a case study written in the first person. Fix by finding the moment the client was wrong, stuck, or embarrassed, and letting it stand without a lesson attached.

**The borrowed posture.** Writing a private operator as a bold leader, or a genuine authority as a humble reporter. The reader cannot name what is off but stops believing the story. Fix by re-reading the transcripts for how the client actually talks about themselves, and matching that instead.

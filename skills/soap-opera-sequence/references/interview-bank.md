# Interview Bank

Ask one beat at a time. Show the scene ledger first so the client sees what you already have and only spends effort on the holes.

Open with these two, always, before any beat questions:

**A.** What did you have, or what were you doing, before the thing that broke? Answer in numbers if you can.
**B.** In one sentence, what actually broke?

---

## Character questions

Ask these only for the elements Phase 2 could not find in the recordings. Most clients supply backstory and parables without being asked, and almost none supply flaws or polarity.

1. What were you doing before this worked? Not the credentials, the job.
2. Is there a story you catch yourself telling people over and over to explain what you do?
3. What are you bad at, that people who work with you already know about?
4. What is the thing about your industry that you think is dishonest, or just wrong?
5. Who do you turn away, and why?
6. Do you want to be out front, or did you end up out front because nobody else was going to say it?

Question 3 is the one that gets deflected. If the deflection is polished, take it, note it as thin, and come back to it once you have a draft. Clients answer this better when they can see what it is for.

Question 6 chooses the archetype. Wanting the front is a leader. Ending up there reluctantly is a reluctant hero. Still working it out is an adventurer. Bringing back other people's answers is a reporter.

---

## Beat 1, Set the Stage

1. What you had, in numbers.
2. How a stranger would have recognized that scale. What could they see.
3. The month or the period it changed.
4. Something true at the time that should not have been possible alongside it.
5. What you were doing to keep it that way. An action, not an attitude.
6. What do you want people to expect from you over the next few days, and how often should they hear from you?

Question 5 is the one that produces the scene. "I wanted to stay under the radar" is an attitude. "There were events in town I skipped so nobody would find out I had a business" is the same thing, and a reader can see it.

Question 6 is the housekeeping half of beat 1. Skip it when a confirmation email already set expectations.

---

## Beat 2, High Drama and Backstory

The email opens at the peak and reverses. Interview in the same order, because clients who start at the beginning talk themselves out of the drama.

**The peak, ask first**

1. What is the worst moment in this story? Start there, not at the beginning.
2. Where were you, and what time of day was it.
3. What was in front of you. A screen, a room, a person, a number.
4. What did you say, or what did somebody say to you.

**The backstory, ask second**

5. How did you end up there? Walk it forward from wherever it started.
6. What the day physically cost you. Hours, sleep, something you stopped doing.
7. What you believed was happening at the time.

**The wall, ask last and do not skip**

8. What was the point where you were stuck and had no answer yet? Before you found anything.
9. What did you try that did not work.
10. What did you still not know.

Questions 8 to 10 matter more than the rest of this beat. That wall is where the reader is standing right now, and the whole parallel of the sequence is built by arranging it rather than stating it. A beat 2 with drama and no wall reads as a war story about somebody else.

Question 6 is the fastest route out of abstraction, and clients almost never volunteer it, because to them it was just Tuesday.

---

## Beat 3, The Epiphany

1. When you sat down to actually look at the problem.
2. What you were counting, comparing, or auditing. What was in front of you.
3. The exact finding. Do not round it.
4. Three specifics that stung.
5. What you did about it.
6. What you expected to break as a result.
7. What actually happened instead.
8. Finish this sentence: I realized ______, and that is when I found ______.
9. The reader's version of that same problem, at their scale.

Question 2 wants an object. Two columns on a page. A dashboard at 3am. A spreadsheet. Something with edges.

Question 8 is the structural one. The second blank is the solution the sequence leads to, and if the client cannot fill it, the epiphany is not connected to the offer and the arc will not land.

---

## Beat 4, Hidden Benefits

1. Name the pieces of what you ended up with. One sentence each.
2. What you expected it to do.
3. What it did that you did not expect.
4. Did anyone say anything to you that they would not have said before. Their words, not your summary of them.
5. Finish this three times: sure, you get ______, but what nobody tells you is ______.
6. For the best one of those, is there a specific time it actually happened to you or a client?
7. What this used to cost, in money or in hours.
8. Why most people cannot or will not do it this way.

Question 4 is the highest-yield question in this bank. Reported speech from a third party is the strongest proof a first-person story can carry, because the client is not the one making the claim.

Question 6 turns a benefit into a parable. A named benefit is a claim. The same benefit as a scene is evidence. Beat 4 needs at least one.

---

## Beat 5, Urgency and CTA

1. What happens in the first week after someone says yes.
2. Proof you still run this yourself.
3. Who should not take this. Say it plainly.
4. Is there a real deadline, a real cap, or a real price change coming? Only real ones.
5. If there is no deadline, what genuinely changes for this person by waiting three months?
6. The exact button text, or the exact ask if there is no button. First person verb.
7. The last line of the sequence.

Question 4 is a yes or no, and a soft yes is a no. An event date, a seat cap they will actually enforce, a price that is actually changing, an asset actually coming down. If the honest answer is that nothing changes, do not invent one. Go to question 5 and run beat 5 on the cost of waiting instead, which is override 3.

When the audience has already converted and there is nothing to buy, replace 1 to 4 with: what does someone walk out of this holding, and what is the one small thing they should do beforehand.

---

## Pushing on a vague answer

Push back once. Take the second answer even if it is smaller.

| They say | Ask |
|---|---|
| "I was burned out" | What day did it get worst. What were you looking at |
| "It was a hard year" | What was the first thing that broke |
| "We were struggling" | What number were you watching |
| "It changed everything" | What did you do differently the next morning |
| "I realized I needed to change" | Who was in the room. What did they say |
| "The results were great" | What did the client say when they saw it |
| "I was doing too much" | What did you stop being able to do |
| "People trust me more now" | What did somebody say to you that they would not have said before |
| "Spots are limited" | How many, and what happens to the person who asks for one after that |

Two rules while pushing.

**Do not lead.** Ask what happened, not whether a particular thing happened. A client will agree with a detail you hand them and then you have invented it together.

**Stop after one push.** A second push turns an interview into an interrogation and the client starts performing. If the second answer is still abstract, mark the beat `[SCENE NEEDED]` and move on. It can be filled later, and an honest hole is cheaper than a soft invention.

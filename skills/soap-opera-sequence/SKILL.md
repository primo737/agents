# Soap Opera Sequence

## What this does

Produces a five-beat serialized email arc in the client's own voice, sourced from things that actually happened to them.

The source framework is Secret #7 of Russell Brunson's *DotCom Secrets*, pages 81 to 94, which Brunson credits to André Chaperon. Secret #6, The Attractive Character, pages 69 to 79, is a prerequisite and is built in Phase 2 here. Secret #8, the Daily Seinfeld Sequence, is what runs after this one ends.

The order of work matters more than anything else in this file. **Mine before you ask.** Most clients have hours of transcripts sitting in their folder, and the scenes in those transcripts are better than anything they will produce on demand in an interview. People summarize when interviewed. They tell stories when they are talking about something else.

Interview only for what the files could not answer.

---

## The failure this skill exists to prevent

A sequence built from interview answers alone comes back as summary. It reads like this:

> I hired people so the work would leave my desk. A business where the owner is the last line of quality is not a business. It is a job with extra steps and a bigger bill.

Correct claim, no scene, nothing for the reader to see. The same client, talking loosely on a discovery call, said he was working seventy two hours a week and could not get to the gym. That detail was in a transcript nobody had mined.

> Seventy two hours a week. I did not get to the gym once.

Same point. One of them a reader can picture.

**The rule:** if a line names a conclusion the reader should reach, it is not finished. Find the moment behind it.

---

## Phase 1: Establish the job

Do not write anything until these six are pinned. Ask only what you cannot infer.

| Question | Why it changes the copy |
|---|---|
| **Who receives this** | Cold list, warm audience, or people who already converted. Changes what beat 5 can ask for |
| **What the sequence is driving** | Registration, application, purchase, or attendance at something already booked |
| **What exists at the destination** | A price, an application, a room. If there is no price anywhere in the client's docs, beat 5 cannot invent one |
| **Fixed date or evergreen** | Evergreen means merge tokens for every date and time, and a fallback for short windows |
| **What else is already mailing** | Every competing sequence in that window. Territory the copy must not reuse |
| **Which house overrides apply** | See the overrides section. Decide before writing, not after a client objects |

If any answer is missing and the files cannot supply it, ask before proceeding. A sequence built on the wrong destination is a total rewrite, not an edit.

---

## Phase 2: Mine the sources

Read every transcript, onboarding doc, and strategy doc the client has. Delegate to parallel agents when there are more than three substantial files. Do not skim.

### Part A: the scenes

Capture these seven verbatim, with a file and location.

1. **Origin.** How they got into this work. What they did before. The pivot.
2. **The moment something broke.** A number that stopped being affordable, a client lost, a partner who took the data, a launch that produced nothing. Where they were, what it cost, what was said.
3. **The wall.** Where the backstory has to arrive: the point they got stuck, before they found the answer. Beat 2 needs this specifically, because it is the spot the reader is standing in right now.
4. **The realization.** What they saw that changed how they work, and what triggered it. Not the philosophy. The trigger.
5. **What arrived that they did not plan for.** Second-order results. These sell harder than the promised one because nobody sells with them.
6. **Dialogue.** Anything anyone said out loud, including what the client said back. Quoted speech is the strongest material in the corpus and it is almost always sitting unused.
7. **Anti-material.** Figures that conflict across sources, results belonging to third parties, anything with no evidence behind it.

### Part B: the Attractive Character

The sequence exists to bond a reader to a person, not to deliver information. Brunson's Secret #6 is the prerequisite and this pass builds it. Without it the emails are competent and forgettable.

Mine for all five. `references/attractive-character.md` carries the detail.

1. **Backstory.** What they were before this worked.
2. **Parables.** The small stories they already tell to make a point. Clients repeat these unprompted across recordings, which is how you find them.
3. **Character flaws.** What they admit to. The thing they are self-deprecating about. This is the element clients resist and it is the one that produces bonding.
4. **Polarity.** What they are willing to be disliked for. Who they refuse to work with. What they call out.
5. **Archetype.** Leader, adventurer, reporter, or reluctant hero. Pick the one the transcripts support, not the one that flatters.

### Part C: voice

Repeated phrases, how they name the problem, how they name the enemy, what they call their audience. Note the register and whether they code-switch.

Build a **scene ledger** before writing a word. `references/scene-ledger.md` has the format.

## Phase 3: Interview the gaps only

Show the client the ledger first: here is what I already have from your own recordings. Then ask only for the beats with no scene against them.

Ask **one beat at a time**. Never dump the full question set. `references/interview-bank.md` holds the questions per beat.

**Push back once on any vague answer.** "I was burned out" is not usable. Ask what day it got worst. Ask what they were looking at. Ask what somebody said. Take the second answer even if it is smaller than the first, because a small true thing beats a large abstract one.

**Never fill a gap yourself.** If no scene exists for a beat, either the beat merges into its neighbour or the draft carries `[SCENE NEEDED: what actually happened here?]` in place of invented colour. A fabricated detail that lands well is worse than a placeholder, because the client will send it.

---

## Phase 4: The five beats

One continuous story. Loops open and close across emails, never inside one. Email 1 pulls to email 2, email 2 pulls to email 3, and so on to the close.

| # | Beat | The move | What it withholds |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Set the Stage | Welcome them, say what is coming and how often, open the first loop | What broke |
| 2 | High Drama, then Backstory | Open at the dramatic peak. Reverse into how they got there. Land at the wall | The answer |
| 3 | The Epiphany | The realization, and what it led to | Nothing structurally. See override 1 |
| 4 | Hidden Benefits | The non-obvious second-order wins, told as parables | The final push |
| 5 | Urgency and CTA | One real reason to move now, and one call to action | Nothing. Close every loop |

### Beat 1

Two jobs, not one. Welcome them and set the frame: who this is, what they will get, and how often. Once a day is the default and it beats every other cadence. Then open the first loop and name what tomorrow's email holds.

**Skip the housekeeping half only when a confirmation email has already done it**, which is the case for show-up nurture on an event someone has already registered for. Say so in the send plan when you skip it.

### Beat 2

This is the beat most rewrites get wrong. **Do not start at the beginning.**

Open at the point of high drama, mid-scene, before any context. Once the reader is hooked, reverse into the backstory that explains how they got there. The backstory must arrive at the wall: the moment they were stuck, with no answer yet.

That wall is chosen deliberately. It is the spot the reader is standing in right now. Weight loss, take them back to being overweight. Money, take them back to being broke. The parallel is never stated out loud, it is arranged.

Then stop. Name that an answer exists and promise it tomorrow.

A chronological beat 1 to beat 2 sequence is the default failure. Stories do not get good until the middle, so start there and backfill.

### Beat 3

The realization, and specifically what it led them to. Structure it as: I saw X, and that is when I found Y. Y is the thing the sequence is selling, or the road to it.

### Beat 4

The wins nobody advertises. The construction is a concession followed by the real payoff: sure, the money, but also the freedom to travel. Sure, the weight, but also being around for the grandchildren.

**Deliver these as parables, not as a list.** A short story demonstrating the benefit outperforms the benefit named. The client's own repeated stories, captured in Phase 2 Part B, are the source.

### Beat 5

One legitimate reason to act now, plus the call to action. Brunson's four working forms: the event starts tomorrow, a real cap on seats or copies, the offer or price genuinely changes, the asset comes down.

**The urgency has to be true.** Invented scarcity costs more credibility than it buys attention. If nothing real exists, either build something real, such as a bonus that genuinely expires, or invoke override 3.

### Formatting, all five

One or two sentences per line. Heavy white space. No long paragraphs. The reader is scanning, not studying.

Write the structural beats first, then fill in the detail and the emotional hooks. Not the other way round.

**Every email closes with a P.S. that names tomorrow's subject line verbatim.** It is the cheapest retention mechanic in the framework and almost nobody uses it.

**Serialize the subject lines** so the arc is visible in the inbox: a short bracketed tag plus a chapter count, then the specific hook. The chapter prefix is exempt from the character limit; the hook after it is not.

## House overrides

Three places where standing client rules override the book. Decide in Phase 1 and write the decision into the send plan. Silently omitting any of them is what this section exists to prevent.

**Override 1: links before beat 5.** The book carries a soft CTA in emails 2, 3 and 4, and ties beat 3's epiphany straight to the offer. Some clients run no link before the close, so the arc holds tension longer and converts later. Default to the book. Flip it when the client's own sequence rules require it, or when the destination is attendance rather than purchase.

**Override 2: the epiphany's landing.** The book resolves beat 3 into the offer. Holding the method back until beat 4 builds more tension and delays the sale. Default to the book. Flip it for long windows and warm audiences who will not leave.

**Override 3: urgency.** The book requires it. Some brands forbid countdowns, seat counters and expiring bonuses on principle. When urgency is off, beat 5 substitutes a single specific ask and a reason that is about the reader's situation rather than the clock. Never fake it in either direction, and never leave beat 5 with no ask at all.

## Phase 5: Variable windows

Skip this phase when the send window is fixed and long enough. It matters for evergreen sequences and anything tied to an event, where people enter at unpredictable distances from the deadline.

**Forward entry, backward exit.** The first email is always a beat 1 asset, so nobody enters mid-arc holding a loop-close for a loop they never received. The last email is always a closing asset at a fixed slot before the deadline, so the arc always resolves. The count between flexes.

Beats 1 and 5 never drop. Beats 2 and 4 are amplifiers and merge first, into the beat they amplify. Beat 3 is the reframe and is the last of the middle to go.

Below the length that fits three emails, write a **standalone** instead: beat 5's job, no loop opened, because one email cannot open and close one. `references/send-architecture.md` has the grid, the fence rule, and the collapse ladder.

## Phase 6: Gates

Run every one and publish the result as a table. A gate that is not published was not run.

**Structure**
- Each of beats 1 to 4 ends on an unresolved loop
- Beat 2 opens at the dramatic peak, not at the beginning, and its backstory arrives at the wall
- Every send path terminates in a closing asset. No dangling loops, no false endings
- No two emails in the same path open the same way
- Every email carries a P.S. naming tomorrow's subject line

**Character**
- The Attractive Character is present, not just a narrator. At least one flaw admitted across the arc
- At least one parable in beat 4
- The archetype is consistent from beat 1 to beat 5

**Copy**
- Every subject hook under 50 characters, count stated per subject, chapter prefix excluded
- One or two sentences per line. No paragraph over four lines
- No opener is a greeting formula
- Every CTA is a first-person verb, specific to that email
- Zero em dashes. English only
- Banned: unlock, leverage, transform, comprehensive, robust, elevate, streamline, empower, optimize, holistic, paradigm, seamless, frictionless
- Plus any word the client's own docs ban

**Truth**
- Every figure traces to a row in the fact table or to the client's evidence file
- No third-party result without permission on file
- Beat 5's urgency is real and the reason is stated, or override 3 is invoked in writing
- No invented scarcity, deadline, number, or client name
- Where sources conflict, the figure is omitted and the conflict is noted

**Telling**
- Scan for lines that name a conclusion instead of earning it. Rewrite before delivering
- The abstraction the sequence is about should not appear as a word anywhere in the copy. A sequence about trust never says trust

**Collision**
- No territory reused from a sequence already mailing that window
- Beat 5 carries only what its audience allows
- Overrides in force are named in the send plan with their reason

Note on polish. Brunson leaves minor grammatical roughness in on purpose, because a character with no flaws does not bond. Do not manufacture errors, and do not sand every sentence into corporate flatness either. Keep the client's own phrasing where it is good rather than upgrading their vocabulary.

## Output

One markdown file in the client's campaign folder.

1. Header: status, audience, destination, mutual-exclusion rule, overrides in force
2. The arc, and why this one
3. The Attractive Character: archetype, flaw, polarity, and the parables in play
4. Beat map
5. The assets, each with subject and character count, preview, body, P.S., cut line, and a one-line note for the operator
6. Gates table
7. **Fact table.** Every claim to its source quote, with file and location
8. Send plan
9. Open calls the client has to make

The fact table is not optional and it is not decoration. It is what lets the client sign off on their own story in ten minutes instead of reading the whole draft with a knot in their stomach.

---

## Delivery

Lead with the work. Then the gates table. Then the open calls, which are the decisions only the client can make: anything self-deprecating in public under their name, anything involving a third party, anything where the transcript is ambiguous.

Do not apologize for a draft. Do not offer option lists after finishing.
